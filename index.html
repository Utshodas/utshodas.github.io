About

I'm a Computer Science graduate (2019-2025) from Shahjalal University of Science and Technology, Bangladesh. I'm interested in usable security and privacy, mostly why people misunderstand systems that are supposed to protect them, and how that shows up in the mental models people build around AI permissions and everyday security tools.

My recent work includes CyQured (SOUPS 2026), a tabletop game I built and tested for teaching personal cybersecurity, and an ongoing project on how people understand permissions and access in AI driven messaging, productivity, and finance apps.

I currently work as an Associate Database Engineer at Therap (BD) Ltd., where I administer Oracle databases for a healthcare platform.

What pulls me toward a PhD is a simple question: why systems that are technically secure so often fail the people who have to use them, and what it would actually take to close that gap.

Research & Publications

CyQured: Design, Development, and Empirical Evaluation of a Tabletop Game for Personal Cybersecurity Education

SOUPS 2026

Designed and empirically evaluated a tabletop game to improve personal cybersecurity awareness through interactive gameplay involving digital assets, cyberattacks, and defense mechanisms.

  • Designed and ran the study — recruited 50 participants split by security background, plus a separate 20-person control group to rule out simple retest effects
  • Handled the full analysis: paired and Welch's t-tests, a linear mixed-effects model to account for group-based play, effect sizes, and thematic coding of interview transcripts
  • Wrote the paper
  • Contributed to game and card design — the STRIDE-mapped attack/defense cards and the Monte Carlo simulation used to place high-risk devices on the board
  • The main finding: even the group that struggled most with the game's mechanics learned just as much as everyone else. High perceived usefulness seemed to carry people through a clunky experience, which says as much about design as it does about learning

A Deep Learning Approach to Automate Classification of Arsenic-Affected Skin using EfficientNet-B1

ICCIT 2024

Achieved 94.69% accuracy in arsenic-affected skin classification using EfficientNet-B1, outperforming VGG-19, ResNet-50, and MobileNet-v2.

  • Built and compared five CNN architectures: VGG-19, ResNet-50, MobileNetV2, and two EfficientNet variants
  • Involved across the full pipeline - data prep and augmentation, training and tuning, and digging into why EfficientNet-B1 outperformed the rest
  • Takeaway that stuck: 94.69% accuracy still meant 71 missed cases - a reminder that headline metrics don't tell the whole story for a screening tool

Understanding End-User Mental Models of AI Agent Permissions Across Everyday Platforms

Ongoing

Investigating how end-users understand AI agent permissions across platforms using interviews and card-sorting tasks to derive safer AI design recommendations.

  • Built the full interview protocol — participants think aloud through four real permission screens (messaging, email/Drive, an AI agent, mobile payment)
  • Designed card-sorting tasks per domain to expose the gap between what people think they're allowing and what they're actually allowing
  • Added controls like counterbalanced scenario order and probes to classify each person's mental model (observer, assistant, delegate, fully autonomous)
  • Currently synthesizing findings into concrete design recommendations for permission screens
